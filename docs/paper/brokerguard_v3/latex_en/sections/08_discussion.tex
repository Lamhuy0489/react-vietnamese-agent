\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why the primary hypothesis failed.} H1 was designed on the assumption that \base{} would let through about
14 of the 48 families, as it did on \tTwo. On \tThree, \base{} let through only 6. The difference lies mainly in sink
leakage: on \tTwo{} this mechanism had 9 harmed family--backbone pairs under \base, on \tThree{} only 1. With such a low
baseline, even if \ours{} blocks every family that \base{} misses, the number of discordant pairs stays very small. At
this baseline the power of the test is only about 26\%, and about 120 families would be needed for 80\% power. The
result on \tThree{} is therefore a floor effect and not evidence that the two configurations are equivalent. Other
sources point in the same direction. On \dev, the number of harmed family--backbone pairs falls from 36 to 4. Rescoring
the 40 cases that once bypassed \base{} on the two earlier sealed sets gives 38 harmful cases under \base{} and 2 under
\ours, both parameter tampering. These numbers, however, come from data used during the development of \ours, so they
are diagnostic only and cannot replace the pre-registered test. No backbone was added and no run was repeated in search
of significance after the results were known.

\paragraph{What each component contributes.} The strongest evidence for \cOne{} and \cTwo{} is at the case level. The
four families that \base{} let through were all blocked by the gate of \ours{} before execution, and each of them gets
through again when exactly the component that blocked it is removed. \cTwo{} addresses the largest gap of \base,
confidential values inside sources labelled public. Under \ours, no value leaks in the final answer. The effect of
\cOne{} is smaller in live runs than in offline checks. On the sink calls of the reference trajectories in the
validation part of \dev, \cOne{} blocks all 35 unrequested side-effect calls, against 15 for \rDest. The models rarely
propose such destinations on their own, however, so on \tThree{} \cOne{} makes a difference for only 2 families.
\cThree{} has no measurable effect on \tThree. The two families that still get through involve changed numbers or
units. Two explanations are possible, and neither has been checked case by case. Either the changed value was not among
the pinned literals, which violates (A5), or the literal was still present next to a different value, a situation that
Proposition~\ref{prop:int} does not exclude. Both point to the need for field-level semantics in \cThree{} rather than a
check on the presence of literals.

\paragraph{Utility cost.} \ours{} loses at most 0.6 points of utility relative to \base{} on either branch, but both lie
about 3 points below \cNone. False blocks are part of the reason. \ours{} falsely blocks 24 of the 960 benign cases of
\tThree, against 19 for \base. On \dev, the most frequent reasons for false blocks are the rules kept from \base{}
(\rFlow{} on source labels and \rSig). For Llama-3.1, \cThree{} comes first, because this model tends to rewrite values
stated by the user when it composes the content to send. Absolute utility is low for every configuration, including
\cNone{} (42.8\%), owing to the limits of small models such as running out of steps or truncated outputs rather than to
the defence. On AgentDojo, the cost is concentrated in tasks that take their destination from tool data. In the
benchmark, \emph{needs confirmation} is enforced as a denial. A real deployment could ask the user at this point, which
is a reasonable way to recover utility without granting authority to untrusted content.

\paragraph{Language.} The \rDest{} rule of \base{} matches strings and is therefore language-independent. \cOne{} is
not, because polarity relies on lists of negation, exclusion and contrast cues for Vietnamese with and without
diacritics and for English. A new language or dialect requires extending these lists, and a negation outside them would
let the gate allow a destination that should have been forbidden. Returning \emph{needs confirmation} when the scope of
a negation is unclear reduces this risk but does not remove it. For detection-based defences the language problem is
more visible still: \cPG{} does not flag a single observation of \tThree, so this classifier does not transfer to the
Vietnamese data of the benchmark.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} Regarding construct validity, the harm predicates and the scorer were frozen before
the runs, but the data are synthetic, the tools are mocks and each task has a single turn. Regarding internal validity,
\tThree{} was generated from a specification by code and reviewed by the same team that developed the defence. The set
was sealed before any \ours{} code existed, but this does not rule out that its authors unintentionally chose cases that
suit the design. The second adaptive set was written by team members, consists of a single round and runs on the
development set. The five supplementary baselines were run after the main results were known. Regarding external
validity, the open models have only 8 to 12 billion parameters, Gemini 3 Flash runs through a proxy without a pinned
version, and AgentDojo was run with a single model.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The propositions are proved on the specification and have not been verified formally against
the code. Property-based testing with a grammar that generates requests and call sequences is a natural next step.
Unauthorised reads are out of scope: under \ours, 18 of the 720 attack cases still contain an unauthorised read call,
almost all on Llama-3.1 (\base: 20). \cTwo{} does not track values through transformations outside (A4), such as
encodings or values split across several calls. The transformed-leak scorer found no such case in the current data, so
this assumption has not really been challenged. Finally, the \cTwo{} detector was designed not to depend on the format
of the secrets produced by the data generator, but its coverage on real secrets still has to be measured on real data.
