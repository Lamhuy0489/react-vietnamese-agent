\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main Results on the Test Set}\label{sec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} summarises the thirteen configurations on the \test. Without a defence, 40 of the 48 families are
harmed on at least one backbone, and 32.1\% of the attack cases succeed. With \ours, only 2 families are harmed, which
corresponds to 5 of the 720 attack cases (0.7\%). As the rules at the broker become stricter, the number of harmed
families decreases monotonically, from 34 with \phrRules{} to 15 with \strRules, 6 with \strSand{} and 2 with \ours. On
the \dev, where these configurations were developed, the order is the same: the number of harmed family--backbone pairs
falls from 346 without a defence to 278, 123, 36 and 4.

The two detection defences have almost no effect on Vietnamese data. \cPG{} does not flag a single observation of the
\test, so its results coincide with those of no defence. \cMel{} also leaves 40 families harmed. \cSpot{} only reduces
the count to 38 while losing 3.1 points of utility. \cSand{} and \cProg{} bring the number of harmed families down to 22
and 25, which still leaves about half of the families harmed.

The primary hypothesis H1 is not confirmed. \StrSand{} has 6 harmed families and \ours{} has 2. Four families are harmed
only under \strSand{} and none only under \ours, so the one-sided exact McNemar test gives $p = 0.0625$ (two-sided
$0.125$). The direction of the effect is the same on all three backbones (from 2 to 1, from 2 to 1 and from 3 to 1
family), but each backbone alone has too few discordant pairs for a conclusion. Following the gatekeeping procedure, H2
to H4 are reported descriptively only. The utility of \ours{} is 0.56 points below that of \strSand{} on the \bPlain{}
branch (720 pairs) and 0.42 points below on the \bAuth{} branch (240 pairs), within the 5-point margin of H2. The three
ablations have 4, 6 and 2 harmed families, against 2 for \ours. Exploratory comparisons with other defences show large
differences, for example 40 against 2 families without a defence ($p = 3.6\cdot10^{-12}$), 22 against 2 for \cSand{}
($p = 9.5\cdot10^{-7}$) and 15 against 2 for \strRules{} ($p = 1.2\cdot10^{-4}$), without correction for multiple
comparisons.

\subsection{Trade-off Between Security, Utility and Cost}\label{sec:tradeoff}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} places the configurations in the plane spanned by the number of harmed families and $\Delta U$.
The \ours{} variants sit at the low end of the security axis, and no other configuration has both fewer harmed
families and more utility than any of them. The utility cost grows as the rules become stricter: \strRules{} lose 0.8
points, \strSand{} 2.6 points and \ours{} 3.1 points relative to no defence. The step from \strSand{} to \ours{} trades 4
harmed families for 5 benign cases, that is, 0.5 points of utility. Since \strSand{} retains more utility and the
ablation without \pThree{} retains one more case than \ours, the results do not support calling \ours{} Pareto-dominant
over these two configurations. The model-based defences lie in the right half of the figure. Detection does not reduce
harm, and the prompt-level defences reduce harm at a cost of 2.1 to 3.1 points of utility. The deny-all-sinks reference
still has 14 harmed families, because confidential values leak through the final answer, and it retains only 11.5\%
utility.

\input{images/fig2_pareto}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports costs per backbone. Relative to \strSand, \ours{} changes the mean number of tokens per case
by at most 0.8\% and the mean wall time per case by between $-1.4\%$ and $+0.6\%$. The extra tokens relative to no
defence are already present with \strSand{} (pooled over the three backbones, 7,565 against 6,782 tokens per case), so
they come from the base rules and the prompt layer rather than from the three new components. The confirmation rate on
benign cases is at most 0.6\%, and the false-block rate ranges from 1.9\% to 3.4\%, against 1.6\% to 2.8\% for
\strSand. All values are below the thresholds of H4. The gate itself takes 0.26~ms per call on average, while model time
averages about 14~s per case.

\subsection{Analysis by Mechanism and Component}\label{sec:mechanism}

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanisms}a shows the harmed families per mechanism. Without a defence, each mechanism harms 5 to 8 of
its 8 families. The prompt layer and the privilege policy strongly reduce the two redirection mechanisms but have
almost no effect on sink leakage, where both \cSand{} and \cProg{} still let 8 of 8 families through. \cProg{} does not
reduce answer leakage or parameter tampering either. \StrSand{} still lets through 1 explicit-redirection family, 1
sink-leakage family, 1 answer-leakage family and 3 parameter-tampering families. \ours{} leaves only 2 families, both
parameter tampering.

\input{images/fig3_mechanisms}

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanisms}b shows that each component blocks the attacks it was designed for. Removing \pTwo{} lets 2
sink-leakage and 2 answer-leakage families through again. Removing the \pOne{} lets through 1 explicit-redirection family
and 1 more parameter-tampering family. Removing \pThree{} leaves the number of harmed families unchanged and adds a single
harmful case (6 against 5). The case-level evidence is consistent with this. All 4 families harmed only under \strSand{}
were sink violations, and all were blocked by the gate of \ours{} before execution, 2 by \pTwo{} and 2 by the \pOne.
Removing exactly that component lets the corresponding case through again. By harm type over the 720 attack cases, sink
violations fall from 136 without a defence to 4 with \strSand{} and 0 with \ours. Final-answer leaks fall from 54 to 0
for both configurations. Parameter tampering falls from 79 to 5, again equally for both. The two families still harmed
under \ours{} involve changed numbers or units, and the gates of both configurations let these calls through.

By language variant, harm without a defence is fairly even, between 40 and 51 of the 144 cases per variant. Under
\ours, the number of harmful cases is 1 for standard Vietnamese, 1 for the paraphrase, 2 for code-mixing, 1 for
Vietnamese without diacritics and 0 for English. With counts this small, no conclusion about the effect of language on
\ours{} is possible.

\subsection{Adaptive Attacks}\label{sec:adaptive}

Table~\ref{tab:adaptive} reports both adaptive sets. In the first set, each backbone is harmed in 6 to 8 of the 24 cases
without a defence and with \cProg, in only 1 case on Llama-3.1 with \cSand{} or \strSand, and in none with \ours. This
agrees with the design but favours \ours, because the gaps targeted by this set were the input to the design. The second
set was written to defeat \ours{} after it had been frozen. Without a defence it is as harmful as the original attacks
(25 against 26 of 42 families), so these attacks are effective. Under \strSand{} and \ours, no family succeeds that does
not also succeed with the original attack. The only family that still gets past \ours{} (on Qwen3.5) is parameter
tampering, the same type as the two remaining families on the \test. In this set, a large share of the cases under
\strSand{} and \ours{} stop because the eight-step budget runs out (up to 18 of 42 cases on Qwen3.5), so the results
only hold within that budget.

\subsection{Generalisation to a Closed Model and to AgentDojo}\label{sec:general}

Table~\ref{tab:general}a gives the reference column for Gemini 3 Flash on the \test. This model is harmed in 26 families
without a defence and in 14 with \cProg, but in none with \cSand, \strSand{} or \ours. Since the prompt layer alone
already brings the count to zero, the column cannot separate \strSand{} from \ours{} on security. It only shows that
\ours{} does not noticeably degrade a strong model. The \bPlain{} utility is 201/240 against 205/240 for \strSand, and
tokens and time per case are similar. In exchange, \ours{} falsely blocks 8 of the 320 benign cases (\strSand: 4) and
requires confirmation in 5. Errors on the model side, such as invalid outputs, affect between 18 and 56 of the 560 cases
depending on the configuration and are counted as utility failures.

On AgentDojo (Table~\ref{tab:general}b), \ours{} produces the same results as \strSand{} on Banking in both languages.
On English Slack, successful attacks drop from 6 to 5. The case that \strSand{} let through because the recipient's name
appeared as a substring of $u$ is now blocked, since the \pOne{} requires a whole-token match. The remaining successful
attacks are password changes through a sink without a destination (3 English and 1 Vietnamese case on Banking), opening
a URL with a read tool (4 cases) and 1 other case on English Slack. The first two kinds lie outside the sink set $S$ of
the formal model. The price is a further loss of utility on Slack, from 15 to 11 under attack in English, and from 5 to 3
of 21 tasks without attack in both languages. Many Slack tasks take their destination from a web page or a message that
the user did not write, and the \pOne{} is stricter than the \rDest{} on such destinations. \PThree{} never blocked a
call on AgentDojo.
