\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Main results.} Table~\ref{tab:main} summarises the thirteen configurations on the \test. With no defence, 40
of the 48 families are harmed on at least one backbone and 32.1\% of the attack cases succeed. With \ours, only 2
families are harmed, or 5 of the 720 attack cases (0.7\%). As the broker rules become stricter, harmed families fall
monotonically from 34 with \phrRules{} to 15 with \strRules, 6 with \strSand{} and 2 with \ours, and the \dev{} column
shows the same order. The detection defences have almost no effect on Vietnamese data: \cPG{} flags no observation of
the \test{} and \cMel{} still leaves 40 families harmed. \cSand{} and \cProg{} block only about half of the families.

For the primary hypothesis H1, harmed families fall from 6 with \strSand{} to 2 with \ours. Four families are harmed
only under \strSand{} and none only under \ours, so the one-sided exact McNemar test gives $p = 0.0625$ (two-sided
$0.125$), short of the registered $\alpha = 0.05$. The effect has the same direction on all three backbones (from 2, 2
and 3 families to 1 each), but each backbone alone has too few discordant pairs. Under the gatekeeping procedure, H2 to
H4 are reported descriptively. The utility of \ours{} is 0.56 points below \strSand{} on the \bPlain{} branch (720
pairs) and 0.42 points below on the \bAuth{} branch (240 pairs), within the 5-point margin of H2. The three ablations
have 4, 6 and 2 harmed families against 2 for \ours{} (one-sided $p$ of 0.50, 0.19 and 1). Against the other defences
the differences are far clearer, for example $p = 3.6\cdot10^{-12}$ against no defence, $9.5\cdot10^{-7}$ against
\cSand{} and $1.2\cdot10^{-4}$ against \strRules{} (exploratory, uncorrected for multiple comparisons).

\paragraph{Trade-off between security, utility and cost.} In the plane of harmed families and $\Delta U$
(Table~\ref{tab:main}\ifpareto, Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}\fi), the \ours{} variants occupy the region with the fewest
harmed families, and no other configuration has both fewer harmed families and more utility. The utility cost grows
with stricter rules, from 0.8 points for \strRules{} to 2.6 for \strSand{} and 3.1 for \ours. Moving from \strSand{} to
\ours{} removes 4 more harmed families at the cost of 5 benign cases, or 0.5 points. Since \strSand{} keeps more utility
and the ablation without \pThree{} keeps one more case than \ours, \ours{} is not Pareto-dominant over these two.
Detection barely reduces harm, whereas the prompt-level defences reduce harm at a cost of 2.1 to 3.1 points of utility.
Even denying all sinks leaves 14 families harmed through the final answer and retains only 11.5\% utility.

On each backbone, the mean tokens per case of \ours{} differ by at most 0.8\% and the mean wall time per case by
$-1.4\%$ to $+0.6\%$ from \strSand. The confirmation rate on benign cases is at most 0.6\%, and the false-block rate is
1.9\% to 3.4\% (\strSand: 1.6\% to 2.8\%), all below the H4 thresholds. The gate takes 0.26~ms per call on average,
against about 14~s of model time per case.

\paragraph{Analysis by mechanism.} Table~\ref{tab:mechres} gives the harmed families per mechanism. \cSand{} and
\cProg{} strongly reduce redirection but still let all 8 sink-leakage families through, and \cProg{} does not reduce
answer leakage or tampering either. Each component of \ours{} blocks the attacks it was designed for: removing \pTwo{}
lets 4 leakage families through again, removing the \pOne{} adds 1 redirection and 1 tampering family, whereas
removing \pThree{} only adds one harmful case. At the case level, all 4 families harmed only under \strSand{} were sink
violations that the gate of \ours{} blocked before execution, 2 by \pTwo{} and 2 by the \pOne, and each case gets
through again when exactly that component is removed. Over the 720 attack cases, the two configurations differ in sink
violations (4 cases with \strSand, 0 with \ours), while final-answer leaks (0) and tampering (5) are the same. The two
families still harmed under \ours{} involve changed numbers or units that both gates let through.

\paragraph{Adaptive attacks.} In the first set, \ours{} is harmed in no case, but this favours \ours, because the set
targets exactly the weaknesses that informed the design. With no defence, the second set is as harmful as the original
attacks (25 against 26 of 42 families), so the attacks are effective. Under \strSand{} and \ours, no family succeeds
with the adaptive attack that does not also succeed with the original one. The only family still getting past \ours{}
(on Qwen3.5) involves parameter tampering, like the two remaining families on the \test. Up to 18 of 42 cases on
Qwen3.5 under \strSand{} and \ours{} exhaust the eight-step budget, so the results only hold within that budget.

\paragraph{Generalisation.} With Gemini 3 Flash on the \test, 26 families are harmed with no defence, 14 with \cProg{}
and none with \cSand, \strSand{} or \ours, so this reference column cannot separate the last two on security. \ours{}
keeps \bPlain{} utility at 201/240 (\strSand: 205/240) but falsely blocks 8 of 320 benign cases (\strSand: 4).

On AgentDojo (Table~\ref{tab:agentdojo}), \ours{} matches \strSand{} on Banking in both languages. On English Slack,
successful attacks drop from 6 to 5: the case in which the recipient's name appeared only as a substring of $u$ is
blocked because the \pOne{} requires a whole-token match. The remaining successes are password changes through a sink
without a destination (3 English and 1 Vietnamese case on Banking), opening a URL with a read tool (4 cases) and 1
other case on English Slack, and the first two kinds lie outside the sink set $S$ of the formal model. In exchange,
utility on Slack drops further, from 15 to 11 under attack in English and from 5 to 3 of 21 tasks without attack in
both languages, because many Slack tasks take their destination from a web page or a message that the user did not
write.
